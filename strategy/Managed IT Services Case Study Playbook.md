# Managed IT Services Case Study Playbook

Oct 7, 2026 · @Sundar Rajan

## How to use this playbook

Every Managed IT Services case study opens and closes the same way. You only choose the middle: answer the questions in the next section, pick Framework A, B or C, and fill in its formulas.

**Fixed on every case study (never changes)**

1. Category tag (Managed IT Services), title, date, "Client identity protected"
2. **Challenge and Solution table**: 4 to 5 rows
3. **Results block**: 2 to 4 figures, shown as Before / Now
4. *Middle sections: from your chosen framework*
5. What changed
6. **Scope disclaimer** (Managed IT case studies only, see its section below)
7. Why we don't name our clients
8. CTA, then Sundar's card

**One rule for the table on IT pages:** at least one Solution cell must name an owner or a runbook, such as "a runbook with a named owner that someone other than the founder can follow at 11 p.m." IT buyers trust accountability more than tools.

**Security rule:** never publish a risk or gap that is still open, or details (software versions, provider names, IP ranges) that could help an attacker. Describe findings by type and impact, and only once fixed or accepted.

**Voice for all IT pages:** calm and forensic. Specifics beat adjectives. No fear-selling: let the facts carry the weight.

## Licensing and wording rules

Pagsun is registered in Singapore for IT services, not cybersecurity services, so case studies must stay clear of the two licensed services. Under Singapore's Cybersecurity Act, only **penetration testing** and **managed security operations centre (SOC) monitoring** are licensable. Providing either without a CSRO licence is an offence with a fine of up to S$50,000, up to two years' imprisonment, or both ([Allen & Gledhill](https://www.allenandgledhill.com/sg/perspectives/articles/21586/sgkh-licensing-framework-for-cybersecurity-service-providers-under-the-cybersecurity-act-2018-effective-from-11-april-2022)). Resellers and third-party vendors supporting these services are covered too ([CSA](https://www.csa.gov.sg/News-Events/Press-Releases/2022/csa-kicks-off-licensing-framework-for-cybersecurity-service-providers)).

| Never write | Write instead |
| --- | --- |
| Penetration test, pen test, ethical hacking | IT estate audit, infrastructure review |
| Vulnerability assessment or scan | Risks or gaps found in IT operations |
| SOC, threat detection, 24/7 security monitoring | Uptime, performance and backup monitoring |
| Breach, ransomware or intrusion response | Outage, server failure, data loss, failed migration recovery |
| "We secured them against hackers" | Access control, patching, backups and policies in place |

**When a client needs a licensed service:** say a licensed security partner handled it, and describe only Pagsun's own part (for example, restoring systems from backup). Refer clients to providers on CSRO's public list of licensees.

**Use the same exclusion line everywhere:** card, proposals, contracts and case study disclaimers, word for word. Matching wording is your strongest evidence of what you do and don't offer.

**Safe practices behind the card**

1. **Contracts and proposals:** a scope clause excluding penetration testing, SOC monitoring and other licensable cybersecurity services.
2. **Refer, don't resell:** point clients to a provider on CSRO's licensee list. Don't bill or take a commission for that work unless CSRO confirms it's allowed.
3. **Security tools:** install and update antivirus or endpoint protection, but threat alerts go to the client or their licensed provider, not to a Pagsun team investigating them as a service.
4. **Monitoring alerts:** health signals only: uptime, disk, CPU, backups, certificates, patch status.
5. **Audits:** configuration, dependencies, backups and documentation. No vulnerability scans, no attempts to break in.
6. **Same words everywhere:** proposals, invoices, LinkedIn posts and sales calls use the card's wording.
7. **Get it in writing:** email CSRO your service list, ask them to confirm none of it needs a licence, and keep the reply on file.
8. **Review once a year:** check whether the list of licensable services has changed.

**Before publishing anything security-related:** confirm with CSRO (contact@csro.gov.sg) or a Singapore lawyer. The Act was amended in 2024, so check whether the licensable services list has changed. This section is guidance, not legal advice.

## Scope disclaimer (Managed IT case studies only)

Every Managed IT case study carries this disclaimer, placed after "What changed" and before "Why we don't name our clients". Fill in the brackets; leave everything else word for word.

> **Scope of this work.** This engagement covered \[IT estate audit / business continuity planning / incident recovery / managed IT operations\] only. Penetration testing, security operations (SOC) monitoring and other licensable cybersecurity services are not part of our Managed IT Services. If a client needs them, we point them to a CSA-licensed provider. Risks and gaps are described by type only, and only after they were fixed or accepted, to protect the client.

**When a licensed provider was involved,** add one sentence: *"Security testing for this client was carried out separately by a CSA-licensed provider; our work covered \[Pagsun's part, e.g. restoring systems from backup\]."*

**What a Managed IT case study can say, and what it can't**

| Topic | Go ahead | Risky: rewrite or remove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monitoring | "We monitor uptime, backups and disk space" | "We monitor for threats / attacks / intrusions" |
| Audits | "The audit mapped dependencies and found 3 single points of failure" | "We scanned for vulnerabilities" or "we tested if it could be hacked" |
| Security work | "We set up MFA, tightened access rights and brought patching up to date" | "We secured them against hackers" or "we hardened them against attack" |
| Incidents | "We restored the server from backup in 4 hours" | "We responded to the breach" or "we contained the ransomware" |
| Findings detail | "A backup process had stopped alerting on failure" (type and impact, fixed) | Software versions, provider names, IP ranges, or anything still unfixed |
| Partners | "We pointed them to a CSA-licensed provider" | "We provided pen testing through our partner" (reads as reselling) |
| Claims | "Documented, tested recovery plan" | "Fully secure", "hack-proof", "compliant" without proof |

## Pick your framework

Ask the questions in order and stop at the first "yes".

1. **Did we respond to something that actually went wrong?** (outage, server or hardware failure, data loss, failed migration) → **Framework B: Back Online**
2. **Is it an ongoing service we run month after month?** (uptime and performance monitoring, patching, backups, helpdesk, cloud and cost management) → **Framework C: Kept Running**
3. **Was it a one-time assessment or plan?** (IT estate audit, infrastructure review, BCP or disaster recovery plan) → **Framework A: What We Found**

|  | A: What We Found | B: Back Online | C: Kept Running |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The story is about... | Risks uncovered before they hit | A crisis handled | Quiet, reliable months |
| Reader | CTO, founder of a SaaS or tech-reliant firm | Owner who just had an incident, or fears one | Business owner with no in-house IT team |
| Reader's silent question | "What could break that I don't know about?" | "If it goes wrong, how fast will they get us back?" | "Will they keep it running so I don't have to think about it?" |
| Results lead with | Findings found and fixed, single points of failure removed | Time to recover, data lost, downtime | Uptime, response time, tickets resolved, cost |
| Central exhibit | Findings table ranked by severity | Incident timeline, hour by hour | 12-month service scorecard |
| Example on site | School ERP audit and BCP | (next case) | (next case) |

**Tie-breaker:** if an audit led to an ongoing contract, use A for the first case study and C for a follow-up a year later. Two case studies about one client show a long relationship, which is itself proof.

## Framework A: What We Found

**Choose when** the work was a point-in-time assessment or plan: an IT estate audit, an infrastructure review, or a business continuity or disaster recovery plan. The reader carries risk they can't fully see. Voice: forensic and calm.

**Title formulas (pick one)**

- *An \[audit type\] and \[plan\] for a \[type of company\]*
  - An IT estate audit and business continuity plan for a School ERP SaaS provider
- *\[Number\] risks nobody had written down: auditing the infrastructure behind a \[type of company\]*
  - 11 risks nobody had written down: auditing the infrastructure behind a School ERP platform
- *What would happen if \[critical system\] failed during \[peak moment\]? A \[plan\] for a \[type of company\]*
  - What would happen if payments failed during fee week? A continuity plan for a School ERP provider

**Table row formula**

- Challenge: *\[Risk\] that nobody had \[documented / tested / noticed\].*
- Solution: *\[Fix, runbook or target\] with a named owner, found during the audit.*

**Results block metrics:** findings found vs. fixed, single points of failure removed, scenarios with a recovery time target, runbooks written, first recovery test passed.

**Intro formula (2-3 sentences):** *A \[type of company\] runs \[critical functions\] for \[their customers\]. They had no documented answer for what happens if \[core piece\] fails during \[peak moment\]. They asked for \[audit\] and \[plan\].*

**Middle sections**

1. **Why we started with \[audit\], not \[plan\]:** the reasoning behind the order.
2. **What the audit covered:** systems and dependencies mapped, in plain words.
3. **What it surfaced:** the findings, ranked. The biggest risks are usually ordinary ones.
4. **The plan, built around real failure modes:** recovery targets, owners, runbooks, and what was prioritised.
5. **Tested:** a drill, a restore test or a tabletop exercise, and what it showed.

**Central exhibit:** a findings table with Finding type, Severity, Likely impact and Status (fixed, planned, accepted). Types only, never exploitable detail.

**Closing line formula:** *\[Client\] now has a documented, tested answer to "what do we do if \[X\] fails", instead of hoping someone figures it out in the moment.*

**CTA formula:** *Not sure your \[plan\] would survive a real outage? Email us and we'll walk through an audit with you.*

## Framework B: Back Online

**Choose when** we responded to something that actually went wrong: an outage, a server or hardware failure, data loss, or a failed migration. The reader has either just lived through an incident or lies awake fearing one. Voice: steady and factual, told in time order. Never dramatise the client's mistake.

**Title formulas (pick one)**

- *Back online in \[time\]: recovering a \[type of company\] from \[incident type\]*
  - Back online in 6 hours: recovering a 40-person trading firm from a server failure
- *How a \[type of company\] lost \[X\] instead of \[Y\] when \[incident\] hit*
  - How an accounting firm lost one hour of data instead of three weeks when its file server failed
- *The first \[time period\] after \[incident\]: what we did, and what we changed so it can't repeat*
  - The first 48 hours after a failed cloud migration: what we did, and what we changed so it can't repeat

**Table row formula**

- Challenge: *\[Incident\] took down \[system\] during \[business moment\], with \[what was at risk\].*
- Solution: *\[Recovery action\] within \[time\], then \[preventive change\] with a named owner.*

**Results block metrics:** time to recover, data lost (hours or records), downtime vs. what it could have been, repeat incidents since (target: zero).

**Intro formula (2-3 sentences):** *At \[time\] on \[day\], \[system\] at a \[type of company\] stopped \[working\]. \[Who\] couldn't \[do what\], and \[what was at stake\]. Here is what happened in the next \[time period\], and what changed after.*

**Middle sections**

1. **The first hour:** what we checked first, and the first decision made.
2. **Stabilising it:** how we stopped the problem reaching more systems and users.
3. **Recovery:** restoring systems and data, in order of business priority.
4. **Root cause:** what actually caused it, described by type only.
5. **So it can't repeat:** the changes made, each with an owner.

**Central exhibit:** an hour-by-hour incident timeline, from first alert to fully restored.

**Closing line formula:** *\[Client\] was back to \[normal operation\] in \[time\], lost \[amount\] of data, and has had \[zero\] repeat incidents since.*

**CTA formula:** *If \[incident type\] hit you tomorrow, how long would you be down? Email us and we'll help you find out before it happens.*

## Framework C: Kept Running

**Choose when** the story is an ongoing service we run month after month: uptime and performance monitoring, patching, backups, helpdesk, cloud and cost management. The reader has no in-house IT team and wants IT to stop being their problem. Voice: reassuring and data-led. Boring is the selling point.

**Title formulas (pick one)**

- *\[Number\] months of \[outcome\]: managing IT for a \[size\] \[industry\]*
  - 12 months without an unplanned outage: managing IT for a 35-person architecture firm
- *How a \[size\] \[industry\] stopped worrying about IT, and cut its costs by \[X\]*
  - How a 20-person clinic stopped worrying about IT, and cut its cloud bill by 28%
- *Running IT for a \[size\] \[industry\] across \[locations\]: one year in numbers*
  - Running IT for a 60-person retailer across 5 stores: one year in numbers

**Table row formula**

- Challenge: *\[IT task\] depended on \[the founder / one staff member / nobody\], so \[issues went unnoticed / fixes were slow\].*
- Solution: *\[Managed service\] with \[response time target\] and a monthly report to \[role\].*

**Results block metrics:** uptime, average response or resolution time, tickets resolved, patch and backup success rate, monthly IT cost.

**Intro formula (2-3 sentences):** *A \[size\] \[industry\] had no IT team. \[IT tasks\] fell to \[person\], who \[had another full-time job\]. Problems surfaced only when \[something stopped working\].*

**Middle sections**

1. **Before we took over:** how IT was handled, and what kept going wrong.
2. **The first 30 days:** what we inventoried, fixed and set up.
3. **What we run every month:** the service, in plain words.
4. **How they know it's working:** the monthly report and what it shows.
5. **One problem caught early:** a short story of something prevented before anyone noticed.

**Central exhibit:** a 12-month service scorecard with uptime, tickets, response time and cost, month by month.

**Closing line formula:** *\[Client\]'s team now spends their time on \[their real work\], and IT shows up only in a monthly report.*

**CTA formula:** *Is IT someone's second job at your company? Email us and we'll show you what a month of managed IT would look like.*

## Results block rules

Show 2 to 4 figures as **Before / Now**, with one small line under them saying how and over what period they were measured. For IT, counts and times are more believable than percentages alone.

| Metric | Before | Now |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single points of failure documented | 0 | 7 |
| Failure scenarios with a recovery time target | 0 | 6 |
| Backup failures that would alert someone | None | Every one |

*Example layout using the School ERP case. Figures are illustrative; use the real counts. Measured at the end of the engagement.*

**No hard numbers yet? Use one of these instead, in this order of strength:**

1. **A tested fact:** "Full restore tested in 2 hours 40 minutes, against a 4-hour target"
2. **A count:** "11 findings: 8 fixed, 2 planned, 1 accepted"
3. **A before/after state:** "Domain renewal moved from one person's memory to a runbook with an owner"
4. **A duration:** "14 months of managed service with no unplanned outage"

"None" or "0" is a valid Before value: it shows the gap honestly. For the School ERP case, add the real findings count and one tested result.
